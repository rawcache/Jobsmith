# Alex Rivera's writing (made up, for the practice copy)

The summer I spent at Northwind Labs changed how I think about data. I walked in expecting to train models and spent my first two weeks staring at duplicate images instead. Nobody had time to clean them, so I built a pipeline that did it for us. It ended up ingesting more than 600,000 images and caught duplicates with 94% precision, which cut the manual review work by about 80%. I'm proudest that the team kept using it after I left.

Ember started as a weekend idea with two friends from my robotics club. We wanted a small robot that could spot fire and smoke before a sensor would. I owned the vision side: I trained the detector, squeezed it onto an NVIDIA Jetson Nano, and kept tuning until inference ran under 200 milliseconds at 30 frames per second. The hard part wasn't the model. It was learning to cut what we didn't need so the whole thing fit on a tiny board.

When our hackathon team at Northwind disagreed about scope, I didn't pick a side right away. I asked each person what they'd demo if we only had one hour left, and wrote the answers on the whiteboard. Two of the three ideas overlapped, so we merged them and dropped the third. We presented to leadership the next morning and won first place. I learned that most arguments about scope are really arguments about what the demo needs to show.

I want to work on hardware and software that has to be fast and has to be right. I like problems where a few milliseconds matter and where you can measure whether you got it. After I graduate I'd like to join a team building perception systems, and grow into someone who can own a system from the sensor to the model.
